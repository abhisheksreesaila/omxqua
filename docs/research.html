the research behind it

why it feels right. and where it's just taste.

Every rule in omxqua comes from somewhere. Some come from studies of real people holding real phones, some from accessibility standards, some from what platforms have learned, and some are simply our taste. Here is each one, labelled honestly, with the source so you can check it.

researcha published study
standardan accessibility or platform rule
practicewhat platforms do, not formally tested
tasteour call, no evidence either way

Touch

Tap targets are at least 44 px (48 px for kids)

researchstandard

Fitts's law says the time to hit a target grows as the target gets smaller and farther away. On phones, Parhi, Karlson and Bederson tested one-handed thumb use and found targets of about 9.2 mm were large enough that people stopped making more errors or slowing down; smaller ones cost both. (For tasks with several taps in a row, about 9.6 mm.) The platform minimums, Apple's 44 pt, Android's 48 dp and WCAG's 44 × 44 px (level AAA), are conventions in the same direction but smaller: 44 pt is about 7 mm. So omxqua's 44 px is a floor, not a target.

In omxqua: every family sets a --oq-target; on phones regular and large buttons and tab bar items never go below 44 px, and Kids get 48. Small buttons (.sm) can be smaller, except Undo in a toast. The 32 px close button has a 44 px invisible hit area. [1][2][3][4][5]

Navigation sits at the bottom of the phone

researchpractice

Steven Hoober watched 1,333 people using phones in streets, airports and buses. Of those touching the screen, 49% used one hand, 36% cradled the phone in one hand and tapped with the other, and 15% used two hands. With one hand, the thumb reaches the bottom and middle of the screen easily and the top corners badly. A caveat: this was observed in 2012–13, on smaller phones, and counts grips; it doesn't measure whether bottom navigation is faster.

In omxqua: the main navigation is a floating tab bar at the bottom on every phone screen; menus and pickers open as sheets along the bottom edge instead of hanging off a button near the top; dialogs put the main action where the thumb is. Never a hamburger menu for main navigation. [6]

Three to five tabs, not more

practice

Apple's guidelines and Material both keep bottom navigation to about five destinations, and put anything else behind "More". The idea behind it is Hick's law, which says choosing takes longer as the number of equally likely options grows; applying it to a tab count is an extrapolation, not a tested result.

In omxqua: the tab bar holds 3 to 5 items; the desktop nav pill shows 4 and folds the rest behind +N. [7][8][19]

Tabs keep their labels; they shrink to icons only while you scroll

researchpractice

Usability testing by the Nielsen Norman Group found that most icons are ambiguous on their own: people guess their meaning wrong unless the icon is nearly universal (home, search). A text label next to the icon removes the guesswork. Apple's tab bars show labels, and iOS 26 adds an opt-in setting that lets a tab bar collapse into a compact form as you scroll.

In omxqua: we first shipped an icons-only tab bar because it looked cleaner, then reversed it the same day after reading this. Now each tab is an icon over a label at rest; scrolling down shrinks it to icons only, scrolling up brings the labels back (our own take on that idea, not a copy of Apple's). The label is always the button's accessible name. [9][8][10]

The tap answers within 100 ms

research

Response-time research going back to Robert Miller (1968), summarised by Jakob Nielsen, puts the limit for "this reacted instantly" at about 0.1 second, and the limit for keeping a train of thought at about 1 second.

In omxqua: every tappable thing squashes the moment a finger lands, before any work happens, so the screen always answers in time even when the result takes longer. Slow work shows a skeleton, never a frozen screen or zeros. [11]

Reading

Text clears 4.5 : 1 contrast, in light and night

standard

WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.4.3 sets 4.5 : 1 as the minimum contrast for body text, so people with low vision or a phone in sunlight can still read it.

In omxqua: every family and every skin is checked by an automated test (tests/contrast.test.cjs) in both themes (adding the skins caught one failure, the Sticker night red, now fixed), including secondary text and status colours on every surface. [12]

Status is never colour alone

standard

WCAG 1.4.1: colour can't be the only way information is shown. Many people have some colour-vision deficiency (most commonly red–green, far more often in men), so "red means failed" alone fails them.

In omxqua: a status badge always carries the word (passed, failed, running) next to its coloured dot. [13][20]

Phone fields use 16 px text

practice

Safari on iPhone zooms the whole page in when you tap a field whose text is smaller than 16 px, and doesn't zoom back. It's well-known browser behaviour rather than a study (Apple doesn't document it), but it is the reason.

In omxqua: fields switch to 16 px text on phone screens.

Frosted glass, only on the navigation

tastethe evidence leans against it

We like how the page shows through the nav as it scrolls. But we should be straight about the evidence: the Nielsen Norman Group's review of Apple's Liquid Glass (iOS 26) found that translucent controls over busy content hurt legibility and pull attention from the content. That is an expert review, not a controlled experiment, but it points one way.

How we limit the cost: glass only on the nav pill and tab bar (cards, menus, dialogs and the Beacon stay solid); a strong 20 px blur and 58% of the surface colour, meant to soften what's behind into colour rather than shapes; the bare logo only frosts once content scrolls under it; and it goes fully solid if the person turns on "reduce transparency" or the browser can't blur. Not yet measured: text contrast on glass over the worst-case content. [14][15]

Motion

The more often something happens, the less it moves

standardpractice

Large or frequent motion can make people with vestibular disorders dizzy or sick; WCAG 2.3.3 (level AAA) asks that motion triggered by interaction can be turned off. Separately, anything repeated many times a minute gets tiring if it animates.

In omxqua: three tiers (Settle for panels, Gentle for menus, None for anything repeated or keyboard-driven); panels and pop-ups never bounce; with "reduce motion" on, every animation becomes a 150 ms fade and the press, logo and helper animations are skipped. [16][17]

Springs, squash and the liquid tab highlight

tastepractice

Springs follow real physics, so motion starts and stops the way objects do, and Apple's designers have argued that this makes interfaces feel connected to the finger. That's a design argument, not a measured result: we know of no controlled study showing springs make people faster or more accurate. We use them because they feel good, and we keep them to small things under the finger (press, thumbs, toggles) where they cost nothing in time.

In omxqua: springs only on press, thumbs, toggles and drag release; the stretch of the tab highlight is our own touch. [18]

The look: families, skins, fonts, colours

taste

Azulejo for travel, Playground for kids, Graphite for developers, the cream background, Geist and Fraunces: these are choices, not findings. Research constrains them (contrast, one accent so the main action stands out) but doesn't pick them.
